Interview record

by Kyle Byrd

This captures one interview — one seat in one loop — in a form that can be analysed afterwards. Filled in consistently across a loop, these records show what each round actually contributed, which interviewers run harsh or generous against the same rubric across every loop they sit on, and whether a fifth interview is telling you anything the first four didn't.

Some fields are here for that longer view rather than for this one hire. The small i beside each one explains what it's for.

When you finish, Save record gives you three ways out: text formatted to paste into your ATS, a JSON file to feed a tracking sheet, or a printable version to email or attach.

The seat

who sat where

What — the ATS candidate ID, not their name. A code keeps the record usable in analysis without carrying personal data around.

Why — this is the join key. Without it a scorecard can't be matched to the other rounds in its own loop, to the hiring decision, or to performance data later. It's the field that turns loose scorecards into a dataset.

What — the req title as posted, and the level as your organisation writes it. Two fields so they stay separable later. Whatever convention you use is fine, as long as it's the one everyone else uses.

Why — the bar is level-specific, and so is loop length. Senior roles take about 37% longer to fill than junior ones, and comparing scores across levels without recording the level produces nonsense.

Keeping level in its own field is what lets you group by it. Buried inside a title string it can't be sorted, filtered or counted without someone cleaning the data by hand first.

Position in loop *

What — which scored round this was, and how many scored rounds the loop has. Round 3 of 5. Count only interviews that produce a scorecard; leave out the recruiter screen and team-match calls.

Why — this is the field almost nobody stores, and it's the one that makes the interesting questions askable. Google's finding that the fifth interviewer adds about one percent of extra confidence could only be calculated because position was known. Without it you can count rounds but never learn which round is carrying the decision.

Bock, Work Rules! (2015), crediting Todd Carlisle
Round of scored rounds

What — your employee ID, not your name. Name is optional below and only there to make the record readable at debrief.

Why — an ID follows you across every loop you ever sit on. That's the point. It means the interviewer becomes something you can look at over time rather than one round on one req, and questions that are currently unanswerable start having answers: does this person pass everyone, does their 3 mean what everyone else's 3 means, do the candidates they championed work out.

Names can't carry that. Two John Smiths collapse into one record, one John Smith splits into four depending on how he was typed that day, and people change their names. Match on names and the pattern you were looking for dissolves into noise before you can see it.

Interviewer-level patterns are usually the biggest finding in any hiring dataset and the most fixable one. Some people run a full point harsher than their peers against the same rubric, for years, and nobody notices because nobody is counting.

What — where you sit relative to the team that's hiring. Pick the closest.

Why — four interviewers who all report to the same manager are four samples of one viewpoint. Both Google and Amazon deliberately place someone from outside the hiring team late in the loop, and Amazon's Bar Raiser exists entirely to occupy that seat. If the last round is always another teammate, redundancy is the expected result rather than a surprise.

What — days between the interview landing on your calendar and the interview happening. Same-day is 0.

Why — this one surprises people. Across 5.1 million interview events, interviewers given one day's notice returned a passing score 68.2% of the time; at seven days' notice it was 63.2%. The candidate isn't the variable. Less prepared interviewers pass more people, which means every team optimising purely for scheduling speed is quietly buying a looser bar.

Ashby Recruiting Coordination report, October 2024. Association, not proven cause.
days
Short notice. At a day or less, scores skew about five points more generous than they do with a week's warning. Worth a second read of your own rating before you submit.

How you scored it

the field that makes or breaks the data
Before writing this, had you heard anything? *

What — whether your score was formed independently, or after exposure to someone else's view of this candidate.

Why — this is the single most load-bearing field on the form. The moment interviewers confer before scoring, every later scorecard is partly an echo of the earlier ones, and no one can work out what each round actually contributed. Answering honestly costs you nothing and keeps the record usable; a contaminated record that looks clean is worse than one that's flagged.

Independent scoring is also the first of the four things Google names as making an interview structured, alongside vetted questions, a shared rubric, and interviewer calibration.

Google re:Work, structured interviewing guide
Flagged as non-independent. The rating still counts for the hiring decision. It just can't be used to measure what this round added, so analysis will exclude it.

The assessment

what you actually saw

What — the attribute from your rubric that this round was designed to test. One per round.

Why — a rubric only works if each round owns a different piece of it. Two rounds testing the same thing is where redundancy actually comes from, and it's invisible unless every scorecard names its attribute. It also means that when the rubric changes, you know which historical records still apply.

Rating *

What — a four-point scale with no midpoint, so you have to come down on one side.

Why — the gap between 2 and 3 is the decision. When interviewer pairs disagree, roughly half the time they're one point apart, and nearly half of those straddle exactly this line. Your 2 and a colleague's 3 is not a rounding difference, it's an argument, and the debrief should treat it as one.

Interviewer pairs agree exactly 62.5% of the time. If your rating differs from someone else's, that's ordinary rather than a sign one of you was wrong.

the line sits here

What — what the candidate said or did that produced your rating. Specifics, not impressions. "Walked through the retry logic and caught the duplicate-write case unprompted" rather than "strong technically".

Why — written detail is the part that has actually been shown to predict outcomes. In a study of 7,650 hires, the number of concrete job-related capabilities an interviewer named in their notes tracked with later performance and promotions, and inversely with turnover. The number on its own carried far less.

Frontiers in Psychology, 2020

0 of 90 characters minimum. Three beats make it useful: what you asked, what they did with it, and what that told you. Describe what happened rather than your impression of it — "caught the duplicate-write case in the retry logic unprompted", not "strong technically".

What — your call. Two options on purpose. If you're torn, pick the one you'd defend out loud and say why in the evidence box; the debrief is where the argument belongs, not this field.

Why — a "maybe" costs someone else the decision you were asked to make, and a loop full of them tells you nothing. Owning the call is also what makes the record worth anything later: scores only prove useful if you can check them against real decisions, and a hedge isn't one.

It's stored separately from your rating deliberately. Keep the score and the call in one field and you can never test whether the scores are driving outcomes or something else is.

Fill this in for candidates you're rejecting too. At organisations under 25 people, scorecards get completed 66% of the time for people who are hired and 47% for people who aren't — and only about 4% of companies contact every rejected applicant. The half of the funnel that says no is the half nobody writes down.

Ashby, 2026; Greenhouse via Axios, 2024

Record complete

Paste this into the scorecard field in your ATS. That's where it can be matched to the rest of the loop, to the decision, and to what happens after the hire. A file sitting in someone's inbox can't be.

What this does — every record you save posts straight into a Google Sheet you own, as a new row. Nothing to copy, attach or chase. That sheet is the tracker: group the rows by candidate reference to see a whole loop, or by employee ID to see one interviewer across every loop they've sat on.

Setting it up takes about five minutes, once. You only need to do it if you're the person collecting the records.

1. Create a new Google Sheet.

2. Extensions → Apps Script. Delete the placeholder code and paste in the collection script.

3. Deploy → New deployment. Set type to Web app, execute as Me, and access to Anyone. Deploy, then authorise when Google asks.

4. Copy the web app URL it gives you. It ends in /exec.

5. Paste it below and press Save URL.

On that "Anyone" setting — the form runs in a browser with nobody logged in, so the endpoint has to accept anonymous posts. Anyone holding the URL can add a row; it does not let them read the sheet. Treat the URL as internal. If a row ever looks wrong, the sheet is yours and you can delete it.

To check it worked, paste the /exec URL into a browser. You should see a short confirmation message. A login screen or an error means the access setting is wrong — redo step 3.

If you change the script later, go to Deploy → Manage deployments and publish a new version. Editing the code alone doesn't update the live web app.

The URL is stored in this browser only, so everyone connects their own copy.
